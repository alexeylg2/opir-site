Before you call the electrician

Four questions that keep you up at night

The answers fit on one sheet of the drawing.

QUESTION 1

“Could my generator hurt a line worker?”

A generator or power station plugged into a socket with a double-plug cable sends voltage back into the grid, where people are working. The drawing shows how to rule that out: the grid and the generator meet only at the changeover switch QS1, I–0–II, which breaks both L and N. They cannot be on together.

QUESTION 2

“Is the inverter big enough?”

Boiler, fridge, pump, lights and router on a backup bus of their own behind the hybrid inverter. The table of groups gives each line’s load; the assistant adds up the backup groups and sets them against the inverter: 1.7 kW against 5 kW, three times the headroom. It also estimates how many hours the battery lasts.

QUESTION 3

“Can the generator run the whole house?”

Usually not, and it is better to know before the first outage. Check Schematic adds up the groups behind the generator’s breaker and warns: 46.7 A on a 16 A breaker. The assistant explains the options: feed the generator to the inverter only, limit the charging current, or switch off the water heater and washing machine while it runs. The choice is yours.

QUESTION 4

“How do I tell the electrician what I want?”

An A3 sheet to ESKD instead of a sketch on a napkin: every device with its designation and rating, wire colours, named lines, a table of groups with cables. Before the visit, ask the assistant to check it: it tells apart what the program sees and what is its own estimate, such as earthing, the inverter’s bypass relay or the pump’s starting current.

In the library

All a backup board needs

Inverter and generator to GOST

Inverter, rectifier and rectifier-inverter to GOST 2.737, generator to GOST 2.722, with + and − and L and N leads, so you draw the actual wiring.

ATS and an I–0–II switch

Automatic transfer switches, single- and three-phase, and a manual changeover with an off position.

Protection at the input

Voltage relays, surge protection, RCDs and RCBOs, so a voltage spike doesn’t burn your appliances and the inverter.

Wire colours and sections

L brown, N blue, PE green-yellow, the battery’s plus and minus red and black. A section on every wire.

Table of groups

Current, the cable’s permissible current and voltage drop for every line, for reference, with the table number of the wiring rules.

“The breaker protects the wire”

Check Schematic spots a cable thinner than its breaker, and groups that together draw more than the feed can take.

Honestly

Safety is the electrician’s job

Opir helps you understand and draw the scheme, but it does not replace a design and does not check safety. It works out sections, permissible currents and voltage drop for reference; short-circuit currents, selectivity and earthing it does not.

The assistant tells apart what the program sees and what the model itself estimates. The board, the generator and the inverter are for a qualified electrician to connect.

Questions

What people usually ask

What if I have a power station, not a hybrid inverter?

A power station is the same rectifier-inverter with the battery inside, and the library has that symbol. Connect it to the board through a changeover switch, as a generator, never with a double-plug cable.

And a three-phase house?

Three-phase breakers, RCDs, a meter, a voltage relay and an ATS are in the library. The manual I–0–II switch is single-phase for now.

Will the assistant draw it for me?

It places the devices from your description and joins the pins into nets. You lay the wires, by hand or with the Route Net command.
