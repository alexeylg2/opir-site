First steps

Four questions every beginner asks

The answers are right in the program, on your own circuit.

QUESTION 1

“What am I even looking at?”

Ask in plain words and the assistant explains your circuit, not a textbook one: C1 charges through R1 and R2, at two thirds of the supply the timer flips, the LED goes out, C1 discharges through R2, and round again. It names every part by its designator on the sheet.

QUESTION 2

“Won’t the LED burn out?”

The assistant does the sums: about 7.3 V at the NE555 output, about 2 V across a red LED, so about 11 mA through the 470 Ω R3, under the 20 mA the LED can take. And it warns that without R3 the LED burns out. It works out the rate too: about 1.5 flashes a second. Where it takes figures from memory because there is no datasheet, it says so.

QUESTION 3

“Did I connect everything right?”

Check Schematic finds an unconnected pin, a wire hanging in the air, a net label with a typo, a chip without power. Click a line and the program shows the place on the sheet and lights up the whole net.

QUESTION 4

“What do I buy?”

The parts list builds itself from the drawing: resistors, capacitors, the chip, the LED, with values and quantities. Print it with the schematic, or save the bill of materials as CSV, and off to the parts shop.

The next circuit

The signal’s path: from the antenna to the earphone

An AM receiver on two transistors. By every node the sheet shows the signal there, so you see a radio wave turn into sound. The signal forms are in the library, 43 of them.

1. The antenna

The wire WA1 picks up every station at once, in microvolts. C1, 10 pF, couples it to the tuned circuit loosely, so as not to detune it.

2. The tuned circuit

L1 and C2 resonate at one frequency: its station stays, the rest fade. Turn C2 to search the medium waves.

3. The detector

VD1 passes one half of the wave, and C3 smooths out the radio frequency. What is left is the envelope, the sound the wave carried, plus a DC part.

4. Volume

R1 takes as much of the sound as you want, and C4 stops the DC part, which would shift VT1’s operating point.

5. Voltage gain

VT1 multiplies the millivolts by about 90. R2 and R3 set the operating point, R5 keeps it steady, C5 gives the audio its gain back.

6. The earphone

VT2 adds no voltage but supplies current: without it a 32–64 Ω earphone would drag the first stage down. C6 lets only the sound into BF1.

What keeps you from getting lost

Finding parts

650 symbols and a search by name: “resistor”, “LED”, “Arduino”, “HC-SR04”. Start typing and the part is already in the list.

Undo anything

⌘Z puts it back, after the assistant too: each of its edits is one step.

A circuit from a magazine

Photograph a circuit from a book and put it under the sheet: draw over it, as on tracing paper.

The datasheet with the part

Drop the PDF on the chip and the assistant takes the pinout from it, not from memory.

Print on A4

A PDF of the schematic and the parts list, to keep by the breadboard while you solder.

Works offline too

Drawing, checking and printing need neither the assistant nor a connection. Only the assistant needs the internet.

Honestly

What Opir doesn’t do

There is no simulation: the program won’t show the LED blinking; that is what a breadboard is for. Circuits on 230 V mains are not for a first try: start with a battery.

The assistant explains and calculates, but it can be wrong, especially where it takes figures from memory. It says where, and the datasheet helps you check.

Questions

What people usually ask

What are R1, C1, DA1?

Reference designators: the letter says what the part is (R a resistor, C a capacitor, DA a chip), the number which one. The program assigns them.

Do I need to know ESKD?

No. Opir draws the frame and title block itself, and a sheet can go without a frame if you like.

Will the assistant draw the circuit for me?

It places the parts from your description and joins them into nets; you lay the wires, and that is how the circuit becomes yours and makes sense.
